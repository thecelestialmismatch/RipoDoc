# RipoDoc MSP Founder Email Outreach Playbook

**Prepared:** 2026-08-15
**Audience:** RipoDoc founder and early MSP sales team
**Objective:** Start thoughtful discovery conversations about one report claim that a client may reasonably ask to inspect
**Primary call to action:** A short **report teardown**—one claim, one source, one reader, one delivery boundary, and one next decision

## Purpose and positioning

This sequence is for founders of Managed Service Providers that already send recurring QBRs, executive reports, or monthly client reports. It is not a generic “replace your reporting stack” campaign. RipoDoc’s evidence-accurate position is narrower:

> **RipoDoc is the controlled evidence-delivery layer for the MSP client report you already send. It is designed to make an approved report claim answerable through bounded, reviewable support for the intended reader.**

The campaign opens a conversation around the moment a client asks, **“Where did that number come from?”** It should not promise that RipoDoc replaces a PSA, RMM, BI tool, or client portal; guarantees time savings, retention, compliance, or ROI; proves arbitrary PDF charts; or enables unreviewed AI publishing. [1]

## Use the sequence responsibly

Personalization must be based on public, relevant business information—not speculation about a person’s private circumstances. Send only where your team has an appropriate basis to contact the recipient, use an accurate sender identity and reply address, avoid deceptive subject lines, honor opt-outs promptly, and follow the recipient’s local marketing and privacy requirements. This is a practical sending discipline, not legal advice.

| Before sending | Required check | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Account fit | The MSP appears to run recurring client reviews, QBRs, account management, vCIO work, or client reporting. | The sequence should invite a relevant conversation, not create noise. |
| One real observation | Find one public, factual detail: their reporting/QBR language, vCIO practice, client portal, growth milestone, or service model. | A specific observation earns attention without pretending you know their internal workflow. |
| Sender identity | Use a real founder name, role, company domain, and monitored reply inbox. | Early founder outreach should feel accountable and human. |
| Clear CTA | Ask only for a short report teardown or permission to send the one-page method. | A small, concrete decision has lower friction than a generic demo request. |
| Opt-out path | Add a simple line such as “If this is not relevant, reply ‘no’ and I will not follow up.” | Treat disinterest as a decision, not an objection to overcome. |

## Personalization worksheet

Write no more than two personalized sentences per account. The message should still make sense if the personalization is removed.

| Field | Good source | Example | Do not do this |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `{{first_name}}` | Public professional profile or business site | `Jordan` | Guess a preferred name. |
| `{{company}}` | Company website | `Northstar Managed Services` | Use a stale acquired-company name. |
| `{{public_observation}}` | Their own page, post, case study, webinar, or job listing | `I noticed Northstar describes vCIO planning and quarterly client reviews as part of its service model.` | “I know your QBRs are painful.” |
| `{{report_question}}` | A likely but optional question, framed openly | `When a client asks which tickets sit behind an SLA number…` | State that a specific client has challenged them. |
| `{{sender_name}}` | Actual sender | `Avery` | Use a fictional founder identity. |
| `{{calendar_or_reply_path}}` | Real scheduling page or reply inbox | `Would a 15-minute teardown next week be useful?` | Send a scheduling link as the only call to action. |

## Sending cadence

Use the sequence over approximately 16 days. Stop immediately after a positive reply, a clear “not interested,” an unsubscribe request, a referral to another person, or a delivery failure. Do not send all messages automatically without a human check of the account and reply state.

| Touch | Suggested timing | Goal | Message length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Day 1 | Name the report-evidence problem and ask a single question. | 75–110 words |
| 2 | Day 4 | Make the “one claim” use case concrete. | 55–85 words |
| 3 | Day 9 | Offer the report-teardown method or sample report; clarify the boundary. | 70–100 words |
| 4 | Day 16 | Close the loop respectfully and give permission to decline. | 45–70 words |

## Sequence A: the report-evidence conversation

### Email 1 — the relevant question

**Subject options:**

| Choose one | Use when |
| --- | --- |
| `A QBR question for {{company}}` | Safest, most direct opening. |
| `When a client asks about a report number` | The recipient publicly discusses QBRs, vCIO, or reporting. |
| `A narrower idea than another client portal` | The recipient already promotes a portal or reporting platform. |

**Email:**

> Hi {{first_name}},
>
> {{public_observation}}
>
> I am working on RipoDoc, a controlled evidence-delivery layer for MSP client reports. The narrow question is what happens after a client sees a QBR metric and asks, “Where did that number come from?”
>
> Rather than send a broad export or move the conversation into another portal, the idea is to make one approved claim answerable with a reviewed, bounded evidence path for the intended reader.
>
> Is that a report moment your team encounters, or not really?
>
> If it is not relevant, reply “no” and I will not follow up.
>
> {{sender_name}}

**Why it works:** It does not claim to know the prospect’s problem, and it asks for a simple truth-seeking reply rather than forcing a calendar decision.

### Email 2 — the one-claim example

**Subject:** `Re: A QBR question for {{company}}`

> Hi {{first_name}},
>
> A concrete example: a QBR says SLA attainment was 97.4%. A client asks which tickets count, what period applies, and who reviewed the support.
>
> The report does not need to become a data dump. But the answer should be more useful than “I’ll send the spreadsheet later.”
>
> RipoDoc is designed around that one-claim workflow: claim, reviewed source, bounded scope, intended reader, and human approval before delivery.
>
> Worth a short comparison against how {{company}} handles that today?
>
> {{sender_name}}

**Why it works:** The number is clearly a fixture example, not a claim about the prospect or a customer result. It shows the product’s operating model in fewer than 90 words.

### Email 3 — the report teardown

**Subject:** `One report question, not a platform replacement`

> Hi {{first_name}},
>
> I am not proposing that {{company}} replace its PSA, RMM, BI tool, or client portal.
>
> The test is smaller: pick one client-facing claim that may be challenged, identify the reviewed source behind it, define the least evidence the reader needs, and name the person who approves delivery.
>
> We call that a report teardown. It produces a decision record for one report cycle—not a transformation project.
>
> If useful, I can send the one-page method or walk through it in 15 minutes. Which would be more useful?
>
> {{sender_name}}

**Why it works:** It proactively handles the most likely category objection: “we already have reporting tools.” It offers a binary next step rather than a vague “let me know.”

### Email 4 — respectful close

**Subject:** `Close the loop?`

> Hi {{first_name}},
>
> I have not heard back, so I will close the loop after this note.
>
> RipoDoc is for MSPs that want a selected client-report claim to be answerable without broadly sharing the operational export behind it. If that becomes relevant, the report-teardown method is here: {{method_or_guide_url}}.
>
> If it is not a priority, no reply is needed. I will not follow up again.
>
> {{sender_name}}

**Why it works:** It protects founder reputation and inbox deliverability. Do not send a fifth “just checking in” email to recipients who have not engaged.

## Sequence B: referral-led version

Use this variation only when the referred person has a clear role in QBRs, client reporting, account management, vCIO, or service delivery.

**Email:**

> Hi {{first_name}},
>
> {{referrer_name}} suggested you may be the right person to ask about {{company}}’s client-reporting workflow.
>
> I am researching a narrow reporting problem: after a client sees a QBR metric, how does the team answer “where did that number come from?” without forwarding a broad operational export or asking the client to navigate a new system?
>
> RipoDoc is a controlled evidence-delivery layer for that selected claim. It is designed to work beside the tools an MSP already uses, not replace them.
>
> Is this worth a 15-minute report teardown, or should I speak with someone else?
>
> {{sender_name}}

## Reply handling

The goal of replies is to learn whether a paid problem exists, not to “win” every objection. Keep every answer short, specific, and accurate.

| Prospect reply | Recommended response |
| --- | --- |
| “Yes, we see that.” | “That is useful context. Which report claim gets challenged most often—SLA, tickets, backup, patching, or something else? If you are open to it, we can map that one example in 15 minutes.” |
| “Send information.” | “Absolutely. The short version is that RipoDoc is designed to attach a reviewed, bounded evidence path to one approved claim in an MSP client report. Here is the method: {{method_or_guide_url}}. If one claim comes to mind after reading it, I would be glad to compare it with your current workflow.” |
| “We already use {{tool}}.” | “That makes sense. RipoDoc is not positioned as a replacement for {{tool}}. The question is whether a client-facing report needs a narrower, reviewable answer when one metric is challenged. Does that happen often enough to investigate?” |
| “We do not run QBRs.” | “Thank you—that is helpful. RipoDoc is best tested where there is a recurring executive or client report. I will not keep following up, but if that changes the method is here: {{method_or_guide_url}}.” |
| “Is this compliance?” | “No. RipoDoc is not a compliance certification. It is designed to make the source, scope, reader, approval, and delivery decision for a selected report claim more explicit. Customers remain responsible for their data, reporting content, and obligations.” |
| “Can it connect to our PSA/RMM automatically?” | “The right answer depends on the source, mapping, approval, and deployment boundary. We should not promise an integration before checking the actual workflow. A teardown can determine whether the claim and source are a fit.” |
| “Not interested.” | “Understood. Thank you for the clear answer. I will not follow up.” |
| No response after Email 4 | Do not reply again. Mark the account closed and preserve only the minimum outreach record your organization needs. |

## The first call: founder discovery script

The email earns a conversation; the call must validate a workflow.

> “Thank you for making the time. I do not want to walk through a generic product demo. I want to understand one report moment: a client sees a metric and asks where it came from. Could we choose one claim from a recurring report and map the current answer—source, scope, reader, and reviewer?”

Use the following agenda. Keep it conversational, and do not ask the prospect to share credentials, customer exports, PHI/ePHI, or other sensitive production information through public marketing tools.

| Minute | Question | What to listen for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–3 | “Which recurring client report matters most?” | A real report cadence and audience. |
| 3–6 | “Which number or claim is most likely to be questioned?” | A specific, not hypothetical, example. |
| 6–10 | “How do you answer today?” | Broad exports, portal handoffs, manual follow-up, or trusted but undocumented practice. |
| 10–13 | “What would the reader need to see to understand the claim?” | Minimum scope and sensitivity boundary. |
| 13–15 | “Who would review and approve that answer?” | An accountable owner and a defined next decision. |

## Metrics and review cadence

A low reply rate does not automatically mean the positioning is wrong; a generic audience, weak personalization, poor list hygiene, sender reputation, or a bad call to action can each be the cause. Review the sequence in account-quality cohorts, not as a single blended number.

| Weekly review | Question | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deliverability | Are messages bouncing, being filtered, or receiving unsubscribe requests? | Stop and repair targeting, list hygiene, and sending practice before increasing volume. |
| Relevance | Which public observations generate replies? | Retain only useful personalization patterns. |
| Problem signal | Which claims do MSPs name without prompting? | Feed exact language into the QBR-evidence guide and future product discovery. |
| Meeting quality | Did the call identify a real report, claim, source, reader, and reviewer? | Prioritize accounts with a concrete report-teardown outcome. |
| Commercial signal | Did the prospect agree to a defined pilot or follow-up decision? | Record the reason; do not interpret polite interest as willingness to pay. |

## Operational checklist

Before sending the first 20 messages, create a small founder-reviewed account list rather than a bulk campaign. For each account, save the source URL for `{{public_observation}}`, the date the message was sent, the sequence step, and the recipient’s reply state. Review the first 10 sends manually to ensure the message still reads as a human note and that every personalization is factual.

The best outcome of the campaign is not a large reply count. It is a small number of real conversations that reveal whether the report-evidence problem occurs frequently enough, carries enough risk or effort, and matters enough to justify a paid pilot.
